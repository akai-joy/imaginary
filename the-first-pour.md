# The First Pour

The Slag Court rang under Sorrel's boots, and she hated that it did.

Black glass, poured and cooled over generations of sworn words, and every step across it sounded like a struck bell announcing her. The Ashwrights lined the rim of the court in their leather aprons and soot-grey hoods, silent, watching the river-clan envoy walk to the center carrying a name that was not hers.

*Sorrel of the Low Fords.* The real Sorrel had drowned two springs ago, and the clans had needed someone the Ashwrights would recognize, someone whose family had stood at a pour before. So they had hung the dead woman's name on her like a borrowed cloak and sent her upriver with instructions she had already decided to ignore. Her people did not want this Accord. Her people wanted the old grievance kept warm — the flooded fords, the diverted water, the winter the smiths let the river-clans starve rather than trade iron for fish.

She wanted it too. That was the thing nobody upriver understood. She wanted the grievance *settled*, which was not the same as wanting it kept.

The matriarch of the Ashwrights waited at the crucible, an old woman with forearms like hawsers and a face that had spent sixty years too close to fire. Since the last spell in the world had guttered out at dusk a season ago, the Ashwrights had become something more than smiths. Wizards ran and hid; oaths poured in metal were the only bindings left that held. Everyone knew it. That was why the court was full.

"Envoy," the matriarch said. "You know the words?"

"I know them."

"Then we pour, and the Accord stands until the metal fails."

The crucible tipped. Molten iron came down in a bright ribbon into the oath-mold between them, and Sorrel spoke her half of the words — water for iron, passage for peace, the fords open and the fires banked — and the matriarch spoke hers, and the court held its breath the way a forge holds heat.

And Sorrel smelled it.

Faint, under the char and the hot-metal reek: something mineral and wrong, like brine dried on stone. She had grown up on the river. She knew the smell of salt the way the Ashwrights knew the color of a true melt.

*Someone has salted the pour.*

It was a saboteur's working, old as the craft itself — a fault seeded into the metal, invisible until the oath cooled, so the ingot would crack in a year, a month, a week, and the Accord would die and each side would swear the other had broken faith. She looked up at the ring of hooded figures and understood that it could have been any of them. It could have been one of her own. Her people had sent her here hoping she would fail; perhaps they had not trusted hope alone.

The words were almost done. In ten breaths the mold would close and the fault would be sealed inside it, and the truce would be born already dying.

Sorrel did the only thing she could think of. She broke the rite.

She stepped forward — the court *rang*, sharp and accusatory — and thrust her bare hand toward the cooling pour, close enough that the heat seared her palm, and shouted over the matriarch's final syllable: "The metal is salted! I contest the pour!"

Uproar. Hoods thrown back, hands on hammers. Contesting a pour mid-rite was a killing offense if she was wrong; the black glass under her feet held the cooled remains of oaths and, it was said, of those who had falsely broken them.

The matriarch raised one hand and the court went quiet. She bent over the mold, and she did something Sorrel had only heard of in stories: she *listened* to the metal, ear close to the shimmering heat, the way old smiths did before the world had needed them to be more than smiths.

Then, far off in the cooling-fields beyond the court, something answered. A long, grinding cry, iron dragged over iron — the Slagmaw Drake, the beast that nested in cooling metal and swallowed oaths whole. It had smelled the fault too. It was coming for the broken thing before the broken thing was even born.

The matriarch straightened. Her face had gone the color of ash.

"The envoy speaks true," she said. "The pour is salted. Bar the gates — no one leaves this court until we know whose hand did it." She looked at Sorrel then, at the blistered palm, at the borrowed name, and something shifted in her expression that was not quite trust but was no longer suspicion. "And bring a second crucible. We pour again at dawn — she and I, alone, with metal I have melted myself."

Sorrel closed her burned hand and felt, for the first time since she had taken a dead woman's name, that she might be allowed to earn her own.

Somewhere beyond the walls, the drake cried again, hungry for oaths that fail.

The Accord would not feed it. Not tonight.
